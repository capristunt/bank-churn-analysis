Bank customer retention: from risk scores to a measurable pilot

A single reading of the four analytical notebooks. This report brings together data quality, customer patterns, predictive performance and the retention decision. It uses their existing aggregate outputs; it introduces no model, test evaluation or targeting choice.

Recommendation: prepare a randomized retention pilot after confirming the operational churn definition, feature timing and campaign economics. The score identifies customers with more departure labels in a retrospective dataset. Whether an offer can retain them remains unmeasured.

The decision in brief

  • Useful retrospective targeting: contacting the highest-scored 20% of the test batch would target 248 of 407 departure labels, using 400 contacts. Precision is 62%, recall 60.9%, and lift 3.05 versus population prevalence.
  • A stronger baseline for a pilot: the frozen business rule targets 169 departure labels and the fixed random draw 85, at the same 400-contact capacity. These are targeted labels, not customers successfully retained.
  • Economics are conditional: the reference assumptions produce €20,800 of simulated net gain for predictive targeting. With no retention effect, the same campaign loses €4,000. No campaign effectiveness or actual return on investment has been measured.

The next decision is to validate the data and test an intervention, rather than treat the simulated gain as a forecast.

1. A usable dataset, with unresolved business timing

The supplied file contains 10,000 rows and 14 columns, with one observed record per customer. Notebook 01 found no missing values, exact duplicates or repeated customer IDs, and no failures in its stated plausibility checks. All rows were retained; no rare profiles were automatically removed. Names, identifiers and row order are excluded from prediction. Zero balances remain valid observations, and a balance is not treated as a margin.

Exited is binary. The Microsoft Learn example describes a matching dataset as account closure; the study infers the educational convention 1 = departure, 0 = no observed departure from its matching structure, sample observations and class counts. This does not certify the supplied file's full provenance, licence or operational definition.

Snapshot and decision dates, label horizon, censoring and feature availability before departure are unknown. Consequently, none of the reported proportions is an annual churn rate, and these results do not establish prospective performance. The data cover customers in France, Germany and Spain, without established representativeness.

The split was fixed before exploring predictor–target relationships: 6,000 training / 2,000 validation / 2,000 test, stratified with seed 42. With unique customers and no dates, this is an exchangeability assumption, not temporal validation. All learned preprocessing is fitted on training data within pipelines; test results remain cached and unchanged.

2. Training patterns suggest questions, not causes

Notebook 02 analyzed only the training set. Inactive customers have a 26.4% departure-label proportion (n=2,901), versus 14.7% for active customers (n=3,099). The age pattern is nonlinear: 48.5% among ages 45–54, compared with 8.1% among ages 18–34. These associations suggest investigating customer journeys jointly with business owners; they do not show that increasing activity or offering another product would prevent departure.

The figure retains the existing 95% Wilson intervals and sample sizes. Age bands follow their natural order. Intervals assume independent customers and omit sampling bias and drift; comparisons are exploratory and not adjusted for multiplicity.

Product count is also nonlinear: the three-product group has 172 training customers and an 82.0% departure-label proportion; the four-product group has just 39 customers, all with a departure label. This small group is not evidence of future certainty. Country differences are descriptive too; commercial scope and potential measurement after departure must be investigated before acting.

The modeling implication was to compare a linear classifier with a tree model capable of nonlinear relationships, rather than turn a marginal segment rate into a causal retention rule.

3. A model selected before reading the test results

Notebook 03 compared a prevalence-only baseline, logistic regression and a random forest. Median/mode imputation, numerical scaling and one-hot encoding were fitted inside training pipelines. Product count was encoded categorically; gender was excluded by design, which does not rule out indirect bias.

The model family was selected by validation average precision (AP), with Brier as a tie-breaker. Sigmoid calibration used five-fold out-of-fold training predictions, with preprocessing refitted inside every fold. It was retained only if validation Brier improved and AP fell by no more than 0.01. Model settings, calibration, the 20% quota, tie-breaking, targeting rules and economic assumptions were frozen before final test evaluation.

Selected on validation: Random forest (sigmoid). On the 2,000-customer test set, ROC-AUC is 0.857 (95% bootstrap interval 0.835–0.877), AP 0.685, and Brier 0.105. Test prevalence is 20.3%; a constant baseline has AP equal to prevalence.

Higher ROC-AUC/AP means better discrimination; lower Brier means lower probability error. Calibration improved Brier on validation, but this does not guarantee a material test benefit or perfect calibration. The model remains the validation choice; there is no new selection from this table.

The 1,000-resample intervals are conditional on the fixed model and omit training/selection uncertainty and drift. Precision at the 20% quota is 62% (95% bootstrap interval 56.0%–67.2%). Missing feature chronology could still overstate future performance.

4. Compare targeting at an identical contact capacity

The business rule ranks 2 × inactive + 1 × age ≥45 years; predictive targeting ranks the selected score; random targeting uses one fixed draw. Ties use seed 42, independently of the target. All three campaigns contact 400 of the same 2,000 test customers. The 20% quota is illustrative capacity, not an economically optimized budget or a fixed probability threshold.

“No contact” is the incremental reference with no campaign cost or gain. Departure labels targeted below are retrospective labels a strategy would reach, not departures prevented.

The fixed random draw targets 85 labels, compared with an expectation of 81.4 under repeated random targeting. The predictive policy concentrates risk more strongly in this holdout; that does not establish stronger response to an offer. Some high-risk customers may be impossible to retain.

5. Economic scenarios expose the conditions for value

Notebook 04 applies the assumptions frozen in 03. They are illustrative, not estimated from campaign data:

Reference assumption Convention
Economic horizon 12 months; the label horizon remains unknown
Cost €2 contact + €8 offer, paid for every contacted customer
Net margin preserved €500 per prevented departure over the economic horizon, before campaign costs
Retention effect q 20% of potential departures prevented among contacts
Capacity and cost 400 contacts; €4,000 total campaign cost

The simulation assumes that the supplied labels represent departure without a new campaign over that horizon, with equal eligibility/reachability, no effect on non-departing customers and no interference. This temporal and counterfactual transfer is unverified. File balances, whose currency is unknown, are never converted into margins.

Simulated net gain = q × preserved margin × departure labels targeted − cost per contact × contacts.

Strategy

At the reference assumptions, predictive targeting produces €20,800 of simulated net gain, versus €12,900 for the business rule and €4,500 for the random draw. At q=0, all campaigns lose €4,000, while no contact stays at zero. These are algebraic scenarios, not measured gains or a validated forecast. Fixed/implementation costs, opportunity costs and longer-term effects remain unspecified; this is not a complete investment case.

With homogeneous positive q and margin M, equal cost c and fixed contact count, expected gain per customer is p × q × M − c, so ranking by risk p also ranks expected gain. That conclusion can fail when response, margin, cost or reachability differs by customer. The study does not estimate those differences. Sensitivity does not change the frozen policy or establish an optimal quota.

6. Turn the uncertainty into a controlled pilot

First confirm provenance and publication rights, define departure and its horizon, establish features known before decision time t0, and validate the score on a dated future cohort. Retention/finance owners must then supply a real offer, contact capacity, payment rules, costs and net contribution measures.

The proposed pilot from notebook 04 would be registered before randomization and outcome observation:

Element Proposed design
Eligible population Open accounts at t0, complete follow-up, no concurrent campaign; initially the top 20% score group after temporal validation
Assignment Customer-level 1:1 randomization to a prespecified uniform contact/offer versus usual-service control; stratify by country and score band
Primary outcome Difference in 12-month net contribution per assigned customer, treatment minus control, by intention to treat; include actual campaign/service costs and realized margin
Success Lower bound of a two-sided 95% confidence interval for net effect above €0; 5% test level
Size and monitoring Plan 80% power for a proposed €10 economically relevant effect; obtain contribution variance before fixing sample size. Prespecify missingness/censoring, incident thresholds and analysis after complete follow-up; no opportunistic significance stopping

The 12-month horizon and €10 design effect are proposals, not measured quantities. Unreachable customers and offer non-acceptors remain in the intention-to-treat analysis. Secondary outcomes include departure, offer uptake, complaints and opt-outs. A pilot restricted to the top-score group identifies effect in that group; proving the best policy for the full customer base would require broader randomized exploration or a randomized policy comparison.

Decision: prepare this measurable pilot after validating the data and operating assumptions. Retain the score as a targeting candidate, without treating risk as uplift or simulated euros as realized value.
